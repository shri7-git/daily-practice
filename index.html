What we're celebrating

It's Krishna's birthday! — the most playful, most-loved little god of all. He was born at midnight, in secret, and the whole world had been waiting for him.

So tonight we stay up a little late, we sing, we make his favourite treats (he loved butter!), and we welcome baby Krishna into our home. In many Tamil houses, families draw tiny footprints from the front door inward — little feet walking in — so the baby god finds his way to us. 👣

"The sweetest, naughtiest, bravest baby in every story — and he starts out just like you: small, and very, very loved."

Names & timing. Janmashtami / Gokulashtami / Krishna Jayanti — the eighth day (ashtami) of the waning moon in Aavani/Shravana; Krishna is said to be born at midnight, under the Rohini star.

Why a baby, why midnight. Krishna is honoured as a purna-avatara, a "full" descent of Vishnu — yet the festival's genius is that it meets you as an infant, born in a prison at the darkest hour: grace arriving exactly where things are lowest and least expected.

The Tamil customs. Rice-flour footprints at the threshold, vella seedai, aval, uri-adi — where each one comes from and what it marks.

"You don't have to teach cosmic theology tonight. You have to help a child fall a little in love with a baby who was worth protecting. The rest grows from that."

Word-for-word: "The divine son of Vasudeva, crusher of Kamsa and Chanura, the supreme joy of Devaki — I bow to Krishna, teacher of the world (jagadguru)."
What it is really saying: You seat Krishna in the heart by remembering him — the one who lifts tyranny, the delight of those who love him, the teacher of all. Each name is a small meditation, not just a fact.

Who she is

Yashoda is Krishna's mother — the one who raised him in Gokul, caught him red-handed with butter on his nose, and loved him so completely it never once mattered that he wasn't born to her. She's in almost every story about baby Krishna.

"You don't have to be someone's birth parent to be exactly the mother they needed."

Vatsalya bhakti. Yashoda is the central figure of vatsalya bhava in the tradition — devotion expressed as parental love rather than formal reverence.

An interlink worth noticing: this rhymes with Vishnuchittar in the Andal node — another parent whose devotion is expressed by raising a found child with total love. Two different festivals, two different sects, the same quiet teaching.

Questions

Questions people ask

Short answers. Where the honest answer is "not yet", it says so.

🪔 What is this, in one sentence?

Five minutes a day — a story, a verse, three breaths — following the Tamil festival calendar through the year.

You can do it with a child or on your own. Both are the normal way to use it.

⏱️ Is it really only five minutes?

Yes. Three things, in order: 1 the story, 2 the shloka, 3 three breaths. That is the whole daily practice and it is the same every day.

There is more underneath if you want it — recipes, longer readings, the libraries. None of it is owed. The five minutes are the thing.

🗂️ We're starting late. Have we missed it?

No. Every day so far is there to read back through, and each one opens complete.

Serials are scheduled so a story never gets cut in half by a festival — so wherever you join, you join at the start of something.

👤 Does a child have to be there?

No. An adult doing this alone is a first-class user, not an afterthought, and the copy is written so nothing assumes a child is in the room.

The Go Deeper tab is a different set of material altogether — longer readings, the fuller verse, the background. It is not the children's version with notes added.

🧒 What age is it for?

Stories are marked with a soft tier rather than a hard age: 🌱 Little ones (~3–6) · 🌿 Most kids (~5–9) · 🌳 Older kids (~8–12).

They overlap on purpose. You know your child better than a number does.

🕉️ Do we have to be religious to use it?

No, and plenty of it doesn't require you to believe anything — the Panchatantra fables, the Tamil poets, the Thirukkural, the breathing.

The rest is told as the tradition tells it, honestly, without asking you to sign up to it. Where a story is specific to one tradition, the source is named on the page.

🌏 Which tradition are these from?

Tamil first — Tamil Nadu and Sri Lankan Tamil practice — with the wider Hindu material underneath it.

Both the Shiva and Murugan strand and the Vishnu and Krishna strand are here, and the calendar is deliberately balanced between them. You will not find a badge on anything telling you which is which. Families are not divided that way in this app.

🚪 Is any of this only for certain families?

No. Everyone is welcome here, whatever your background, whatever your family's name.

You will notice that a good many of the stories chosen make exactly that argument themselves — a foundling girl, a hunter, a robber, a farm labourer, an old woman. That is not an accident.

✅ Has an expert checked all this?

Not yet, and that is the honest answer. Everything here is drafted first, then checked. A full review by someone who actually knows the tradition — content and every festival date against the panchangam — happens before this goes to real families.

If you know better than we do about something, please tell us. That is genuinely the most useful thing you can do here.

📅 Are the dates right?

Treat them as provisional. Festival dates follow the lunar calendar and shift each year, and every date in here still needs confirming against a panchangam.

Check with your own temple or almanac before you plan anything around one.

💔 Some of these stories have deaths in them.

They do. The tradition does not have a version without them, and pretending otherwise tends to come apart the first time a child hears the real one somewhere else.

So every story that involves a death, a killing or a disappearance carries a Read this first note before the story starts, with a plain answer ready for the question a child is most likely to ask.

You get to decide. The note is there so you can decide before you start reading aloud, not halfway through.

🔊 Is there narration?

🔜 Coming soon — not built yet. Every story is written to be read aloud by you. That is the real thing here — a recorded voice will be a layer on top, not a replacement.

🍲 Do we need to cook something?

Never. Do it Together is the fun part, not homework — and the five minutes are complete without it.

Festivals show up several days ahead so that if you do want to make something, you have seen the ingredients in time.

🔨 What isn't built yet?

No accounts, no sign-in, no payment. No real streak — the row of days at the top of a day screen shows what falls when, not what you have done.

Some pages are still marked soon, and most of the "links to…" mentions inside stories are not tappable yet.

This is an early draft shown early on purpose.

The 💬 button, bottom right, on any screen. It goes to one person and nobody else.

What helps most: what confused you, what your child actually asked, and anything that looked wrong.

Why this exists

I grew up on stories — from my grandmother, from my parents, from whatever I could get hold of and read. Children growing up here are surrounded by different things. I didn't want the thread to go quiet just because we moved.

So: five minutes a day. A story, a verse, three breaths. Not a class, nothing to keep up with, nothing to fall behind on.

Why this exists

Why this exists

The honest version, in as much detail as you want it.

⏳ Five minutes, every day

Five minutes a day beats an hour once a month. Not because five minutes is a lot — it obviously isn't — but because the every-day part is what compounds.

1% better every day · 1% worse every day

That illustration comes from James Clear's Atomic Habits: improve by 1% a day and you end the year roughly 37 times better off; slip by 1% a day and you end it near zero.

It's a metaphor rather than a measurement — nobody can actually quantify being 1% better at anything. But the shape of the curve is the honest part, and it's the whole reason this asks for five minutes instead of an hour.

The flat, boring beginning is the part most things don't survive. So there's nothing here to keep up with, and no streak to lose.

🌬️ Why breathing

Three breaths, every day, at the end.

The mechanism is real and well described: breathing out slowly is what slows the heart. A long exhale shifts you toward the calming side of the nervous system, and you can measure it. That part isn't in dispute.

What I won't claim is that three breaths a day will make your child calmer. The research on breathing programmes for children is genuinely mixed, and some large trials have found no effect at all.

What it reliably does is smaller, and I think still worth it: it gives a child a named thing they can do when they're upset — something they already know, that they don't have to be taught in the moment. That's the honest version of the promise.

📖 Why stories, verses and food

These are the three things that actually carried the tradition into my life, so they're the three things here.

Stories, because that's how any of it reached me — told, not taught.

Verses, because a shloka is a small thing to learn and it stays with you for decades. You don't need to understand all of it the first time. Nobody does.

Food, because it's the part most families still do. Cooking something together on the day is not a lesser version of observing it. Often it is the observance.

🌏 Growing up away from where this came from

This is the part I care most about.

A child who knows where they come from has one less thing to work out later. Not to wall themselves off — the opposite. It's easier to be open to everything around you when you're not quietly unsure about your own background.

Roots and reach aren't in tension; the first makes the second cheaper. I'd rather children take the best of where they live and know what's behind them, than have to pick.

🙏 What this is, and isn't

This is an invitation to be curious about a tradition, not an instruction to believe anything.

You do not have to be observant, or Hindu, or sure about any of it. Anyone is welcome here. There's no requirement, no ranking, and nothing you have to have been born into.

It's meant to be a useful, honest resource — that's all.

🤖 Who wrote this, and who checks it

I'm a student of this, not a teacher of it. I practise, I read, and I get things wrong.

Yes — I drafted this with AI. Nothing reaches a family until someone who knows the tradition has checked it, and that pass hasn't happened yet. That's why you're seeing it labelled as a draft rather than presented as finished.

The festival dates are the one thing I've started checking against a published panchangam, because a wrong date is the fastest way to lose your trust. Several were wrong. They're corrected now.

If something here looks wrong to you, please tell me. That's partly why you're seeing it this early.

Skanda Sashti

Soorasamharam · the day Murugan ends a long fear
📅 Sun, Nov 15, 2026🦚 six days of build-up
⏱️

Today, in five minutes: the last part of the story, Murugan's shloka, three breaths.

📚

Beyond the five minutes. Not part of tonight's practice, and not in any order.

✨

What we're marking

For six days, Murugan has been getting ready. Today is the sixth.

Murugan is the young one — a boy god with a peacock, a spear, and no fear at all. Today is the day he finally faces Soorapadman, who had made everyone afraid for a very long time. 🦚

"The bravest one in the story is also the youngest one in the story."

Names & timing. Skanda Sashti / Kanda Sashti / Soora Samharam — the sixth day (sashti) of the waxing moon in Aippasi, kept most intensely at Tiruchendur.

Why six days. The festival is a countdown, not a single event — six days of fasting and recitation building to the confrontation on the sixth. The Kanda Sashti Kavasam is recited throughout.

Whose day this is. Murugan is the centre of Tamil Saiva devotion, and for many Sri Lankan Tamil families he is the god of the household — Kataragama as much as Palani.

⚠️ Read this before the story

Today's story ends with Soorapadman being killed. It is the whole premise of the festival, so it cannot be edited out — but you get to choose how much detail your child hears. The telling below stays on fear ending rather than on violence.

If they ask "did Murugan kill him?" — "Yes. Soorapadman had made himself into someone who could not be talked to any more, and he was hurting everybody. Murugan stopped him." Then, if it helps: in many tellings Soorapadman asks to stay near Murugan afterwards, and becomes his peacock — the story ends with the enemy carried, not discarded.

Part 6 of 6 · ~4 min
The last of the six days

For five days you have heard how afraid everyone was. Soorapadman had taken everything and frightened everyone, and nobody could stand in front of him.

So the gods did something unusual. They did not send their strongest. They sent a boy — Murugan, six-faced, born from Shiva's own light, carrying a spear called the vel that his mother Parvati had given him.

Murugan did not shout. He arrived at Tiruchendur, by the sea, and he simply did not move when Soorapadman came at him. That was the thing nobody had managed before: to stand still in front of the fear.

And then it ended. The long fear ended.

Afterwards — and this is the part people love — Soorapadman asked not to be sent away, but to stay beside Murugan always. So he became the peacock Murugan rides. The thing everyone had been terrified of ended up carrying him.

"He didn't win by being the biggest. He won by not running."

Thought spark"What is something that used to scare you, that doesn't scare you any more?"

Reading

What the six days are actually doing

The Soorasamharam is read on at least three levels, and Tamil Saiva commentary moves between them freely rather than choosing.

As a story. A demon king, a boon he abuses, a young god sent because the old ones cannot manage it. Standard puranic architecture, and the same shape as Mahishasura at Navaratri and Narakasura at Deepavali. Reading the three together in one year is one of the more useful things this calendar can do.

As inner work. Soorapadman is routinely read as the ego — specifically as pride that has grown past correction. On this reading the six days of fasting are not preparation for watching a battle; they are the battle, and Murugan's stillness is the instruction.

As place. This is not an abstract festival. It happens at Tiruchendur, on the shore, and the sea is part of the telling. Murugan's six houses (arupadai veedu) map the story onto actual Tamil geography — a tradition you can stand in.

The Kavasam. The Kanda Sashti Kavasam, recited through the six days, is armour-poetry — it asks for protection limb by limb. For many Tamil families, including Sri Lankan Tamil households, it is the single most-recited devotional text in the house.
